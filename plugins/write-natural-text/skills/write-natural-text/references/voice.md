# Voice and level

Use this guide for voice and genre choices; use `ai-tells.md` for the final check.

## Match the writer

Explicit user requirements take priority over a supplied sample, and a sample takes priority over the default voice. Match sentence length, openings, transitions, vocabulary, formality, and genuine humor or asides. Keep punctuation within the task's formatting rules. A sample does not authorize invented facts, opinions, or experiences. Without a sample, use the draft's genre and the default below; neutral factual text stays neutral.

## The default speaker

Write as a person who can discuss the subject but does not need to sound like a senior consultant. The reference picture is a fourth-year university student writing in English: they understand the topic, explain their own view with ordinary words, can give an example, admit a doubt, or make a mild joke. Their English is clear enough for a course answer or a work post.

The "fourth-year" detail matters: do not turn the text into a child's answer. Keep the reasoning and the useful technical details. Explain them in simpler sentences.

The voice is not a biography. Do not add facts about the speaker's age, university, country, or job unless the user supplied them. Do not imitate a nationality through broken grammar.

Useful qualities:

- A clear opinion attached to a particular situation.
- Common words and normal contractions: "I'd", "don't", "that's".
- Some changes in sentence and paragraph length.
- Examples that someone could picture without reading the assignment again.
- Doubt about a particular decision, rather than a general disclaimer about everything.

These are editing tools, not a new template. Do not force one opinion, one contraction, and one doubt into every paragraph.

## Keeping English at B1

Prefer familiar verbs: use, help, choose, check, build, keep, find, change, ask, fix. Do not replace ordinary words with formal ones to sound professional.

| Heavy wording | Simpler wording |
| --- | --- |
| utilize | use |
| facilitate collaboration | help people work together |
| prioritize implementation | decide what to build first |
| ensure stakeholder alignment | check that the teams agree |
| mitigate potential issues | reduce the risk of problems |
| conduct a comprehensive evaluation | check the main parts |
| leverage AI capabilities | use AI |
| subsequently | later |
| authored, relocated, attempted, commenced | wrote, moved, tried, started |

Choose replacements by meaning. "Check" is not always enough to describe a formal approval. Keep the detail that matters.

Use mostly straightforward sentence forms. Split a sentence when it has several conditions or side points. There is no fixed word limit per sentence; making every sentence equally short creates another visible pattern.

Keep exact technical terms such as API, SQL, encryption, and load testing. Explain unfamiliar ones through what people do; do not replace them with vague words to lower the level.

Keep ordinary grammar. Do not add spelling errors, broken articles, fake foreign expressions, or random filler. A text can sound natural and still be correct.

### Connectors at B1

A B1 writer links ideas with a small set of words. Cycling through formal connectors is a visible upgrade.

- Prefer: and, but, because, so, then, also, for example, if, when, still, however (rarely).
- Avoid, or use once at most: moreover, furthermore, additionally, in addition, consequently, thus, therefore, hence, notwithstanding, that said, nevertheless, subsequently, "Firstly ... Secondly ... Finally", "In today's world".
- No metatext that announces what the text will do: "Let's look at the next aspect", "This answer will explore", "Now let's turn to", "In this post I want to share", "As mentioned above".
- Do not open by restating the question. Start with the answer or with the situation.
- Repeat the topic noun instead of cycling synonyms. "The reminder ... the reminder ... it" is what a real B1 writer does. "The reminder ... this tool ... the feature ... the solution" is elegant variation, an old machine tell.

## Patterns that make simple words sound prepared

### Every paragraph has the same job

A typical weak answer follows the same order every time: define the topic, list the advantages, list the disadvantages, give an example, end with a one-line summary. Each paragraph looks like a box in the grading criteria, and the same shape repeats across several answers.

Still cover everything the question asks. Check coverage privately, then let the explanation follow the subject. One practical example may show both an advantage and its limit; those points do not need separate labelled paragraphs. Do not simply shuffle the parts and call the text revised.

### Too many explicit cause-and-effect links

Brief:

> Customers can move a delivery to a later time in the app instead of calling the shop. The writer dislikes making that call.

Weak draft:

> Moving a delivery in the app supports the goal of avoiding a call to the shop.

This spells out the feature-to-benefit link mechanically. When drafting from the brief, describe the supplied situation:

> If I know I'll be late, I want to move my delivery to a later time myself. Calling the shop just for that is annoying.

"Because" is allowed. The problem is repeatedly spelling out the same feature-to-reason-to-benefit chain. Do not replace every "because" with "therefore" or "as a result".

### Repeated first-person starts

"I chose", "I also chose", "I would add" in a row. Keep first person when it carries a real choice or opinion. Let other sentences start with the example, the feature, or the problem. Read adjacent paragraphs together to catch the rhythm.

### Safe statements that fit any answer

Before:

> Planning delivery times needs careful testing.

> The scenario does not include real data about customers.

The first is vague. If the brief identifies a booking problem, explain that problem:

> The app might let a customer pick a delivery time when no driver is free. The shop needs to see that before the customer gets a confirmation.

Without that detail, ask or keep the statement general; a style rewrite cannot invent the failure mode.

The second can be necessary for honesty, but must not become a standard closing sentence. If the question asks for evidence, say what the choice is based on in the relevant part of the answer. Do not remove uncertainty by inventing research.

### Simple words with a polished structure

A text can use basic vocabulary and still feel like a corporate answer: perfect transitions, equal paragraph roles, no loose ends, consistently tidy reasoning.

Allow a short comment to stand on its own. Some ideas need more space than others. An answer does not always need a final lesson or summary. If the last useful point is clear, stop there.

Do not manufacture self-corrections, repetitions, or awkward phrases to create an uneven surface. The aim is a person's way of explaining a real thought, not a performance of poor writing.

## Keep the facts while changing the voice

A natural voice does not justify fake customer interviews, survey results, work history, or measurements. Keep assumptions visible, but place them where they matter:

> I guessed two drivers per evening, because the scenario doesn't give a number.

That is more useful than ending every answer with a broad disclaimer. For an imagined example, use conditional language:

> Suppose customers keep moving their delivery to a later time.

Do not turn that into "Our customers kept complaining about delivery times" without evidence.

When a question asks for a reflection, write a proposed response grounded in the available material. Do not add a university story or a customer meeting to make the voice feel personal. If the user gives a genuine reaction, keep its meaning and tone rather than replacing it with bland praise.

## Match the kind of text

### Course answers

Read the actual questions, scenario, and any examples needed for a comparison question. Each answer must make sense by itself, so put the relevant choices in the answer instead of pointing to another document.

Use question numbers, but do not give all answers the same opening, paragraph count, or ending. One may begin with a user problem, another with a concern about the plan. Do not enforce variety through a fixed rotation of openings either.

For a comparison, name a real similarity and difference in the supplied examples. Do not write generic statements about "effective strategies" without showing what differs.

### A short post

Keep the personal point close to the announcement. Do not expand it into a manifesto or add hashtags and motivational slogans without a reason. Example of length and tone:

> We stopped doing daily stand-up calls last month and moved to short written updates in the team chat.
>
> I thought people would miss the calls. So far nobody has asked to bring them back. The updates are easier to find later, and I get twenty minutes of my morning back. When something is actually broken, I still prefer a quick call, because typing it all out takes too long.

A reference for length and tone, not a paragraph to reuse.

### One-line additions and mild criticism

If the user asks for one extra line, return the line without a long introduction or several unrequested options. When editing an existing sentence, keep earlier requested points unless the user replaces or removes them. Keep mild criticism when the user asked for it; do not soften it into praise or make it stronger than intended. Example:

> The written updates only help when everyone remembers to post them.

### Replies in a conversation

When the thread is available, begin with the answer, decision, or request. Omit background the recipient supplied or already accepted. Keep new facts, necessary reasons, and details needed to act; do not remove them merely to make the reply short. If the audience or context is unclear, retain enough background for the text to stand alone.

Given a message that already explains why a delivery date must move:

> Before: You explained that the boxes will arrive on Tuesday, so Monday is no longer possible. We can deliver on Wednesday. Does that work?
>
> After: We can deliver on Wednesday. Does that work?

This omission is appropriate for the reply, not for a standalone delivery notice.

### Posts for other platforms

The platform changes the length, not the voice. A tweet or a Telegram message is one or two plain sentences with the point first. An Instagram caption can be personal and short; it does not need a hook line or a hashtag wall. A Facebook post reads like something said to friends. In every case: start with the fact or the situation, say what the writer thinks, stop. No "Excited to share", no emoji bullets, no "Agree?" closer, no hashtags unless the user asks for them.

### Emails and letters

Say why the writer is writing in the first sentence or two. Use the greeting and sign-off the user names; when none is given, keep them plain ("Hi <name>," / "Thanks, <name>"). One request per email where possible. No "I hope this email finds you well", no "Please do not hesitate to contact me", no closing summary of the email itself. A cover letter follows the same rules: concrete things the writer did, one or two reasons this job, no list of adjectives about themselves.

### Presentation text

Slide text is a few short lines, each carrying one fact or one claim, in the same plain words as the rest. Speaker notes can be full sentences in the same voice. Do not turn every slide into three bullets of equal length, and do not add a title-case heading that repeats the first bullet.

### Longer articles

A detailed article needs more space, not more formal English. Group related ideas, use examples, and let the amount of content decide paragraph length. Do not make every section follow introduction, three balanced points, and a summary. Headings help readers find information in a long guide; they are not the default shape for a personal post.
